# Wildcards: the guide's own agenda

She doesn't know what she doesn't know. Most new grads choose from the five jobs their classmates applied to. The guide's job is to show her the rest: industries and roles where her actual skills are valued and that she would never have searched for. Every industry round carries two wildcards, and every scouting run includes one discovery run.

## What makes a good wildcard

- **True:** people with her background really are hired there (you can point to a posting, a team page, or an alum).
- **Surprising:** not on a careers-fair list for her degree.
- **Different:** varies at least one axis from what's already on the map (setting, mission, kind of work, size).
- **Concrete:** you can describe a Tuesday.

Not a wildcard: a random job she isn't qualified for, a "pivot to tech bootcamp" cliché, or a slight rewording of something already rated.

## Five ways to generate them

1. **Skill leaps.** Take one transferable skill and ask who pays for it outside her field. Precise measurement → metrology, calibration labs, forensic labs, anti-doping labs, art conservation science. Surface chemistry → cosmetics, paints and coatings, adhesives, food texture, contact lenses. Explaining science → patent firms, medical communications, museum and science-centre programming, technical training.
2. **Follow the money and the rules.** New regulations and big public spending create hiring: battery and critical-minerals supply chains, water infrastructure, PFAS testing and remediation, carbon accounting and verification, food-safety rules, drug and device approvals. Search for what's newly funded or newly regulated in her region.
3. **The supplier behind the famous company.** Every big company has suppliers that hire the same skills with less competition: instrument makers, reagent and materials suppliers, contract research and testing labs, equipment service firms.
4. **The translator roles.** Jobs that sit between scientists and someone else: application scientist, technical sales, regulatory affairs, patent agent trainee, science policy analyst, grant officer at a research funder, technology transfer at a university, venture-capital analyst for deep tech, journal editor.
5. **Ask the job market.** Search recent postings for her core skills (for example "Raman spectroscopy", "thin films", "electrochemistry", "M.Sc. chemistry") and note which sectors the employers are in. Any sector that shows up and isn't on the map is a wildcard candidate.

## Discovery runs (part of scouting)

A scout brief of type "wildcard discovery" asks the scout to:

1. Run method 5 for her top three skills, in her location (and remote).
2. Run methods 2 and 3 once each for her region.
3. Return up to five industries **not already on the map** (the brief lists the map's industries), each with `origin: wildcard`, `status: fog`, a blurb, a `why_her`, and at least one source showing someone with her background hired there.
4. Optionally, two or three example organisations per wildcard, with sources.

## Presenting a wildcard

Mark it as the guide's pick, with the reason in one line: "My wildcard: **art conservation science**. Museums hire chemists to work out what paintings are made of and how to stop them decaying, often with the same Raman spectroscopy you used." Then let her react like any other card. Don't sell it.

When a wildcard is rated *drawn*, say so with some delight. When several wildcards in a row land *no*, ask what they had in common; that's a push worth knowing.
